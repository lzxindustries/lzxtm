## Overview

Vertigo is a real-time affine rotozoomer — a classic demoscene effect that maps a procedurally generated repeating texture onto an infinite plane, then continuously rotates and zooms the viewing window across it. The name evokes the disorienting visual sensation of watching a textured plane spin and breathe, and the Alfred Hitchcock technique of simultaneous zoom-in and track-out that creates a nauseating spatial distortion. Six procedural textures — XOR, AND, checkerboard, stripe, dot, and woven — provide a library of repeating patterns, each with its own visual character when subjected to rotation and scale modulation.

The rotozoomer requires no input video. It is a pure synthesis program that generates its output entirely from mathematics: a DDS phase accumulator drives the rotation angle, a second accumulator oscillates the zoom factor sinusoidally, and per-pixel texture coordinates are computed via an accumulator walk that requires zero multiplications per pixel after the per-line initialisation. This efficient architecture — a staple of 1990s demoscene programming — produces fluid, full-resolution animation from minimal FPGA resources: no BRAM at all, just combinatorial logic and a 256-entry elaboration-time sine lookup table.

At gentle settings, Vertigo produces a meditative, slowly rotating pattern with subtle zoom pulsing — suitable as a generative background or ambient visual source. With high rotation speed, deep zoom oscillation, and the blocky quantisation mode engaged, it becomes an aggressive tribute to the DOS demo era, rendering a Mode 13h–resolution XOR fractal spinning at maximum velocity.

## Background

### The Demoscene and Real-Time Synthesis

The demoscene is an international computer art subculture focused on producing demos — self-contained programs that generate audio-visual presentations in real time. Originating in the Commodore 64 and Amiga cracking scenes of the 1980s, the demoscene evolved into a legitimate art form with competitions (demoparties) judged on technical achievement and aesthetic impact. The rotozoomer was one of the first "wow" effects — demonstrating that a home computer could transform and animate a 2D texture in real time, years before hardware 3D acceleration became available. Vertigo brings this effect to the analog video domain.

### Affine Texture Mapping

Affine texture mapping applies a linear transformation to map texture coordinates onto screen coordinates. For a rotation by angle $\theta$ with scale $s$, the texture coordinates $(tx, ty)$ at screen position $(x, y)$ are:

$$tx = s \cdot (\cos\theta \cdot (x - cx) + \sin\theta \cdot (y - cy))$$
$$ty = s \cdot (-\sin\theta \cdot (x - cx) + \cos\theta \cdot (y - cy))$$

where $(cx, cy)$ is the centre of rotation. The crucial optimisation is that adjacent pixels on the same scanline differ by a constant delta: $\Delta tx = s \cdot \cos\theta$, $\Delta ty = s \cdot \sin\theta$. This means the per-pixel computation reduces to a simple accumulator addition — no multiplications needed after the per-line initialisation. This "accumulator walk" technique made rotozooming possible on 1990s hardware without floating-point units.

### Procedural Textures from Bitwise Operations

The XOR texture — formed by computing $tx \oplus ty$ for each texture coordinate — is perhaps the most iconic pattern of 1980s computer graphics. Its self-similar fractal structure emerges entirely from the bit-level interference between the two coordinate axes. The AND texture ($tx \wedge ty$) produces the Sierpinski triangle fractal. Checkerboard, stripe, dot, and woven patterns are generated by testing individual bits of the coordinates, creating repeating geometric structures at different spatial frequencies. All six textures are purely combinatorial — they require no memory, no lookup tables, and no state.

### DDS Phase Accumulators

A Direct Digital Synthesizer (DDS) generates a periodic waveform by continuously incrementing a phase accumulator and using the accumulated phase to index a lookup table. Vertigo uses two DDS accumulators: one for rotation angle (driving the sine/cosine lookup for the affine transform) and one for zoom oscillation (driving a sinusoidal scale modulation). The increment rate of each accumulator is controlled by the Rot Speed and Zoom Speed knobs respectively. Because the accumulators are 16 bits wide, they naturally overflow and wrap, producing smooth, continuous periodic motion without explicit modulo arithmetic.

### Mode 13h and Blocky Aesthetics

Mode 13h (320×200, 256 colours) was the standard VGA graphics mode used by DOS games and demos from 1987 through the mid-1990s. Its chunky, low-resolution pixels became an iconic aesthetic — each pixel was visible as a distinct square block on CRT monitors. Vertigo's Blocky mode emulates this by masking the lower bits of the texture coordinates, effectively quantising the texture lookup to a 4× coarser resolution. The result is a deliberately pixelated rendering that evokes the aesthetic of Second Reality, Unreal, and other landmark DOS demos.

## Signal Flow

```
Synthesis (no input video required)
│
├── Frame Tick (vsync) ──────────────────────────────────────
│   │
│   ├─ 1. Advance rotation angle: angle_phase += Rot Speed
│   ├─ 2. Advance zoom phase: zoom_phase += Zoom Speed
│   ├─ 3. Compute cos(angle) and sin(angle) from sine LUT
│   ├─ 4. Compute scale: Base Scale + Zoom Depth × sin(zoom_phase)
│   ├─ 5. cos_s = cos × scale, sin_s = sin × scale
│   └─ 6. Update texture scroll offset
│
├── Per-Line Init (hsync) ───────────────────────────────────
│   │
│   └─ 7. Compute starting tx/ty from line y-position and cos_s/sin_s
│
├── Per-Pixel Accumulator Walk ──────────────────────────────
│   │
│   └─ 8. tx_accum += cos_s, ty_accum += sin_s (per pixel)
│
├── Texture Coordinate Extract ──────────────────────────────
│   │
│   ├─ 9. Extract tx, ty from accumulator high bits
│   └─ 10. If Blocky: mask lower 2 bits → 4× quantisation
│
├── Texture Generation ──────────────────────────────────────
│   │
│   └─ 11. Select texture: XOR / AND / Check / Stripe / Dot / Woven
│
├── Colorize ────────────────────────────────────────────────
│   │
│   ├─ 12. Scale texel (8-bit) → luma (10-bit)
│   ├─ 13. If Video Tint: modulate luma by input Y
│   └─ 14. Map Tint Hue → U/V colour offset
│
├── Mix ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────
│   └─ Interpolator: dry (input video) ↔ wet (synthesis)
│
└── Bypass ──────────────────────────────────────────────────
    └─ Select input video or synthesised output
```

The pipeline is structured around three temporal scales. Frame-level computation (at vsync) updates the rotation angle, zoom oscillation, and scroll offset — these change once per frame and define the global transform for the current frame. Line-level computation (at hsync) initialises the texture coordinate accumulators for the start of each scanline, computing the left-edge texture position from the current row's y-position and the frame's rotation/scale parameters. Pixel-level computation simply adds the constant per-pixel delta to the accumulators — the cheapest possible per-pixel operation.

The zero-multiply-per-pixel property is what makes this effect practical on the iCE40's limited DSP resources. The only multiplications occur once per frame (cos × scale, sin × scale) and once per line (initialising the accumulator start positions). The actual per-pixel rendering is pure addition and bitwise operations — the texture generation itself is combinatorial logic with no arithmetic at all.

## Parameter Reference

<img src={vertigo_control_panel} alt="Videomancer front panel with Vertigo loaded"/>
*Videomancer's front panel with Vertigo active. Knobs 1–6 (top two rows of left cluster), Toggle switches 7–11 (bottom row of left cluster), Fader 12 (right side).*

### Rotary Potentiometers (Knobs 1–6)

#### Knob 1 — Rot Speed
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 37.5% |
| Suffix | % |

Controls the angular velocity of the texture rotation. At zero, the texture is stationary — no rotation occurs. As Rot Speed increases, the texture rotates faster, spinning the infinite plane around the screen centre. At moderate values, the rotation is smooth and hypnotic; at maximum, the texture spins rapidly, creating a stroboscopic blur effect. The Direction toggle determines whether rotation proceeds clockwise or counter-clockwise.

---

#### Knob 2 — Zoom Speed
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 37.5% |
| Suffix | % |

Controls the rate of sinusoidal zoom oscillation. At zero, the zoom factor is static — set entirely by Base Scale. As Zoom Speed increases, the texture alternately zooms in and out at a faster rate, creating the characteristic "breathing" pulsation of demoscene rotozoomer effects. The oscillation is sinusoidal, producing smooth acceleration and deceleration at the zoom extremes.

---

#### Knob 3 — Zoom Depth
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 50.0% |
| Suffix | % |

Controls the amplitude of zoom oscillation. At zero, no zoom pulsation occurs regardless of Zoom Speed — the texture remains at the scale set by Base Scale. As Zoom Depth increases, the zoom oscillation swings between progressively larger and smaller scales, creating deeper breathing cycles. At maximum, the texture zooms from a very wide view (small pattern elements) to a very close view (large pattern elements) each cycle.

---

#### Knob 4 — Base Scale
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 50.0% |
| Suffix | % |

Sets the static zoom level around which the sinusoidal zoom oscillation centres. At low values, the texture is zoomed far out with small, densely packed pattern elements. At high values, the texture is zoomed far in with large, coarse pattern elements. This control and Zoom Depth together define the zoom range: the texture oscillates between Base Scale − Zoom Depth and Base Scale + Zoom Depth.

---

#### Knob 5 — Tint Hue
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0deg – 360deg |
| Default | 0deg |
| Suffix | deg |

Selects the colour tint applied to the synthesised texture. The hue maps around a colour wheel: 0° produces warm reds/oranges via high V and low U offsets, progressing through yellows, greens, cyans, blues, and magentas as the angle advances through 360°. The tinting is applied as fixed U/V offsets that colour the monochromatic texture luminance. At any position, the texture gains a distinctive colour cast derived from the 8-entry hue lookup table.

---

#### Knob 6 — Scroll
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 25.0% |
| Suffix | % |

Controls the speed of a persistent vertical and horizontal texture offset drift. At the midpoint (50%), no scrolling occurs. Below midpoint, the texture scrolls in one direction; above midpoint, it scrolls in the opposite direction. The scroll accumulates over time, continuously drifting the texture origin. Combined with rotation and zoom, scrolling adds a translational component to the otherwise purely rotational/scalar motion, creating more complex and less predictable visual trajectories.

---

### Toggle Switches (Switches 7–11)

| Switch | Off | On |
|--------|-----|-----|
| **7 — Texture** | XOR | AND |
| **8 — Direction** | CW | CCW |
| **9 — Blocky** | Off | On |
| **10 — Video Tint** | Off | On |
| **11 — Bypass** | Off | On |

The toggles configure the visual character and motion parameters of the rotozoomer. Texture selects which of the six procedural patterns is rendered. Direction controls rotation sense. Blocky and Video Tint modify the rendering quality and colour source. Bypass provides instant comparison with the unprocessed input video.

---

### Linear Potentiometer (Fader 12)

#### Fader 12 — Mix
| Property | Value |
|----------|-------|
| Range | 0.0% – 100.0% |
| Default | 100.0% |
| Suffix | % |

Crossfade between the dry (input video) and wet (synthesised texture) signals. At 0%, the output is pure unprocessed video. At 100%, the output is the full rotozoomer synthesis. Intermediate values blend the procedural texture over the input video, creating a superimposition effect where the rotating pattern overlays the source at partial opacity.

## Guided Exercises

These exercises explore the rotozoomer from basic spinning textures through complex multi-parameter compositions, demonstrating how the six procedural textures and motion controls interact to create the classic demoscene aesthetic.

### Exercise 1: Classic XOR Spin

<img src={vertigo_exercise1_result} alt="Classic XOR Spin result"/>
*Classic XOR Spin — simulated result across source images.*
**Objective**: Create the iconic demoscene XOR rotozoomer with smooth rotation and gentle zoom breathing.

1. **Select XOR texture**: Set Texture to XOR. The characteristic fractal interference pattern appears.
2. **Start rotation**: Increase Rot Speed to ~40%. The texture begins spinning smoothly.
3. **Add zoom breathing**: Set Zoom Speed to ~25%, Zoom Depth to ~40%. The pattern pulsates rhythmically in and out.
4. **Set base scale**: Adjust Base Scale to ~50% for a balanced zoom level showing moderate pattern detail.
5. **Tint the pattern**: Rotate Tint Hue to explore different colour casts — cyan, magenta, gold — each dramatically changes the mood.
6. **Compare textures**: Cycle Texture through all six patterns, observing how each responds differently to rotation and zoom.

**Key concepts**: XOR produces the most visually complex self-similar pattern under rotation, zoom breathing creates the classic demoscene pulsation, tint hue colours the monochromatic texture

### Exercise 2: Mode 13h Nostalgia

<img src={vertigo_exercise2_result} alt="Mode 13h Nostalgia result"/>
*Mode 13h Nostalgia — simulated result across source images.*
**Objective**: Recreate the chunky, pixelated rotozoomer aesthetic of DOS VGA demos with blocky quantisation and fast rotation.

1. **Enable blocky mode**: Toggle Blocky to On. The texture immediately coarsens into visible pixel blocks.
2. **High rotation speed**: Push Rot Speed to ~75%. The blocky pattern spins rapidly.
3. **Deep zoom oscillation**: Zoom Speed ~50%, Zoom Depth ~70%. Aggressive zoom breathing amplifies the chunky aesthetic.
4. **Try checkerboard**: Select Checkerboard texture. The binary pattern creates stark, high-contrast blocks under blocky quantisation.
5. **Warm tint**: Set Tint Hue to ~30° for an amber/orange cast evoking CGA/EGA era monitors.
6. **Add scroll drift**: Increase Scroll to ~60%. The texture drifts diagonally while rotating and zooming.

**Key concepts**: Blocky mode emulates Mode 13h resolution, checkerboard creates maximum contrast under quantisation, scroll adds translational motion to the rotational animation

### Exercise 3: Video-Modulated Woven

<img src={vertigo_exercise3_result} alt="Video-Modulated Woven result"/>
*Video-Modulated Woven — simulated result across source images.*
**Objective**: Use Video Tint mode to project a woven mesh texture through the input video, creating a composite where the pattern is visible only in bright areas.

1. **Select woven texture**: Set Texture to Woven. The interlocking stripe pattern appears.
2. **Enable Video Tint**: Toggle Video Tint to On. The pattern is now modulated by the input video's luminance.
3. **Slow rotation**: Rot Speed ~15% for a gentle, ambient spin.
4. **No zoom**: Zoom Speed 0%, Base Scale ~60%. Static zoom for clarity.
5. **Blue tint**: Set Tint Hue to ~240° for a cool blue colour cast.
6. **Partial mix**: Set Mix to ~60%. The woven pattern overlays the source at partial opacity, creating a textured composite.
7. **Try other textures**: Switch to Dot and Stripe to see how different patterns interact with the video luminance.

**Key concepts**: Video Tint modulates synthesis luminance by input video Y, creating a composite where the pattern follows the source brightness, partial mix blends synthesis and source for layered compositions

## Tips

- **XOR is king**: The XOR texture produces the most visually complex and hypnotic pattern under rotation. Start with XOR to explore the parameter space, then switch textures for variety.
- **Zoom Depth and Base Scale are partners**: Base Scale sets the centre of the zoom range, Zoom Depth sets the amplitude. A high Base Scale with moderate Depth produces gentle, close-up breathing. A low Base Scale with high Depth creates dramatic wide-to-close zoom swings.
- **Scroll for asymmetry**: Pure rotation and zoom are symmetric — the texture spins around the centre. Adding Scroll breaks this symmetry, making the motion path more unpredictable and less repetitive.
- **Blocky for retro**: Blocky mode is the single most effective control for evoking the DOS demo aesthetic. Combined with fast rotation and deep zoom, it creates an aggressively nostalgic visual.
- **Video Tint for compositing**: Use Video Tint to project the procedural texture through a camera feed, creating live video-modulated patterns. Best with high-contrast sources where brightness varies across the frame.
- **CCW for freshness**: Most viewers unconsciously expect clockwise rotation. Counter-clockwise adds a subtle unfamiliarity that can enhance the disorienting vertigo effect.
- **Low Rot Speed as ambient**: At very low rotation speed (5–10%) with gentle zoom breathing, Vertigo produces meditative ambient patterns suitable for background projection or installation work.
- **Woven for textile effects**: The Woven texture at half-scale with mild rotation creates convincing animated fabric patterns, especially when tinted with warm earth tones.

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Accumulator Walk** | A technique where each pixel's texture coordinate is computed by adding a constant delta to the previous pixel's coordinate, eliminating per-pixel multiplications. |
| **Affine Transform** | A linear geometric transformation (rotation, scaling, translation) applied to map texture coordinates onto screen coordinates. |
| **Bayer Matrix** | An ordered threshold array used for dithering; referenced in the context of retro rendering techniques. |
| **DDS** | Direct Digital Synthesizer; a phase accumulator that continuously increments to generate periodic waveforms (here: rotation angle and zoom oscillation). |
| **Demoscene** | An international computer art subculture focused on producing real-time audio-visual demos, originating in the 1980s cracking scene. |
| **Mode 13h** | VGA graphics mode (320×200, 256 colours) used by DOS games and demos; its chunky pixel aesthetic is emulated by Vertigo's Blocky mode. |
| **Phase Accumulator** | A register that continuously adds an increment value, wrapping at overflow, used to generate periodic signals with DDS. |
| **Rotozoomer** | A real-time effect combining rotation and zoom of a 2D texture, a staple of 1990s demoscene productions. |
| **Sierpinski Triangle** | A fractal pattern produced by the AND operation on coordinates; one of Vertigo's six procedural textures. |
| **XOR Texture** | A procedural pattern generated by bitwise XOR of horizontal and vertical coordinates, producing a self-similar fractal interference pattern. |
| **YUV** | A colour encoding separating luminance (Y) from chrominance (U, V), used throughout the Videomancer pipeline. |
